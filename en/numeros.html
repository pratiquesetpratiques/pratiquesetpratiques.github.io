1. Sébastien Marchand, "Histoire de la Macédoine hellénistique," Guides bibliographiques & documentaires, 11 p. 10.5281/zenodo.18961476.

Abstract
This bibliography offers a gradual introduction for non-specialists interested in the history of the Kingdom of Macedonia, a region in northern Greece whose most famous symbol and ruler was Alexander the Great. It focuses on the Hellenistic period of the kingdom: less, therefore, on Alexander's reign itself (336-323 BCE) than on the new era he ushered in, whose key players for Macedonia were the kings of the Antigonid dynasty, who occupied the throne for most of this period (277-168 BCE). The references provided deal primarily with political history, but also seek to cover all aspects of society (economy, war, religion, culture, etc.) and all the documents used to study it (literary, archaeological, numismatic and epigraphic sources, etc.).

Also freely available on HAL.science.

2. Sven Günther, "Journal of Ancient Civilizations (JAC), or How to place an Afro-Eurasian antiquity journal in the scholarly market," Librarians & Editors for Ancient Studies. Voices, 19 p. 10.5281/zenodo.19171355.

Abstract
This paper delineates the development of the Journal of Ancient Civilizations (JAC) — published by the Institute for the History of Ancient Civilizations (IHAC), Northeast Normal University, Changchun, China — and its contributions to the scholarly world. JAC has transformed from an institute's network-based platform into an internationally recognized scholarly publication which serves to bridge the gap between Chinese scholars studying the Ancient Afro-Eurasian world and the latest international research. This paper reviews the transformation process and addresses the challenges currently being faced by the integrated institution-journal-identity model in a rapidly changing journal market which trends towards digital publishing.

3. Luiza Osorio G. Silva, "Ancient Egyptian Kingship," Guides bibliographiques & documentaires, 11 p. 10.5281/zenodo.19307243.

Abstract
This guide introduces readers to the fundamentals, essential developments, and complementary developments of ancient Egyptian kingship studies. Ancient Egyptian kingship, a complex facet of ancient Egyptian society, remains a topic of fascination for both scholars and the wider public. Its study has traditionally focused on the nature of kings and related topics such as legitimacy and cosmology. However, in recent years scholars have started to recontextualize kingship within broader ancient Egyptian society, seeking to understand how the royal institution was engaged with by a variety of actors. This guide traces some of these developments and provides readers with an overview of the origins of scholarship and the current state of the field.

4. Carlos Gracia Zamacona, "Dealing with ancient Egyptian mortuary texts: why and how to study an ancient culture in the 21st century," Librarians & Editors for Ancient Studies. Voices, 38 p. 10.5281/zenodo.19461944.

Abstract
Ancient Egyptian mortuary texts are ad-hoc, in-context productions that were in constant adaptation to changes in mentality, specific production situations, and use interests, rather than established textual units. For this reason, their variability is so high that the need for digital techniques to assess this fundamental feature is evident to anyone familiar with this material. This article briefly presents the problems, aims, and strategies proposed by the MORTEXVAR project and related projects run at the University of Alcalá.

5. Carlos Carvalhar, "Bibliographie essentielle sur Platon et l'Égypte," Guides bibliographiques & documentaires, 14 p. 10.5281/zenodo.20418510.

Abstract
The main objective of this article is to provide quick access to an essential bibliography on Egypt in Plato. Indeed, this theme is only addressed within a niche field of study that is frequently overlooked. This concerns both the historicity of Plato's journey to Egypt and the debate about Egyptian thought. Regarding the latter, while there are explicit mentions within the dialogues, we must not ignore the implicit traces, which are even less discussed. We will briefly highlight the myth of Atlantis, the critique of writing in the myth of Theuth, and other themes such as eschatology, politics, and the arts.

7. So Miyagawa, "Building an OPAC for a Specialized Ancient Near Eastern and Egyptological Library in Japan: The UTsukuba RCWAC OPAC," Librarians & Editors for Ancient Studies. Voices, 34 p. 10.5281/zenodo.20020475.

Abstract
This paper describes the design, implementation, and deployment of a quadrilingual (Japanese, English, Arabic, German) open-source Online Public Access Catalogue (OPAC) for the Research Center for West Asian Civilization (RCWAC) at the University of Tsukuba. The RCWAC library holds over 9,000 volumes spanning cuneiform studies, West Asian archaeology, and Egyptology, including donated collections of considerable scope. Faced with the challenge of making this multilingual, multiscript collection discoverable without dedicated server infrastructure or institutional IT support, we developed a fully client-side single-page application built with Vite, powered by Zotero RDF data, and deployed at zero cost on GitHub Pages and a university Apache server. The system features token-based fuzzy search with optional semantic similarity via ONNX Runtime Web, a four-section transliteration keyboard for Egyptological and Assyriological special characters, right-to-left layout support for Arabic, and prominent call number display to bridge digital discovery with physical shelf access. By situating this project within the broader landscape of digital library platforms for ancient studies—from large-scale initiatives such as the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI) and the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae (TLA) to the Lib-Static methodology for sustainable digital collections—we discuss the practical choices, technical trade-offs, and lessons learned that may inform similar efforts at other specialized research libraries.

8. So Miyagawa, "Egyptological and Ancient Near Eastern Studies in Japan: Key References Available in Japanese Libraries," Guides bibliographiques & documentaires, 13 p. 10.5281/zenodo.19771814.

Abstract
This bibliographic guide surveys the principal Japanese-language publications and reference works for Egyptological and Ancient Near Eastern studies that are available primarily or exclusively in Japanese libraries, with particular attention to the holdings of the Research Center for West Asian Civilization (RCWAC) library at the University of Tsukuba. 9. Edson Poiati Filho, "Bibliothèque ouverte des mondes anciens (BOMA): Initial Milestones of an Independent Open-Access Non-Profit Publishing Initiative," Librarians & Editors for Ancient Studies. Voices, 21 p. 10.5281/zenodo.19255097.

Abstract
Bibliothèque ouverte des mondes anciens (BOMA) is a non-profit publishing initiative conceived in France during the summer of 2025. This paper presents the initial milestones reached after several months of development. It describes the two foundational pillars of BOMA: 1) a zero-cost operating model and 2) an editorial policy that encourages experimentation. It discusses the legal framework, hosting and indexation, long-term online preservation, licensing, scientific authority, publishing tools, and visual identity, before outlining future perspectives.

10. Simon Thuault, "Egyptology and Social Media: the YouTube Experience Initiative," Bridging Ancient Worlds, 23 p. 10.5281/zenodo.22215581.

Abstract
Ancient Egypt, and Egyptology by extension, have long been popular subjects and field of study. Exhibitions, documentaries, books, artistic productions… Whenever Ancient Egypt is presented, the general public is drawn to it, or at least intrigued by it. From a more positive perspective, we can consider that this popularity is a great way to spread knowledge about Egyptian civilization. Nonetheless, as light means shadow, this popularity has always fostered the emergence of conspiracy theories, fake revelations, ideological fights, and so on. Social media intensified this state of affairs, especially since Egyptologists have been late taking on this new medium, thus allowing people with varying degrees of malicious intent to occupy this space without being challenged or debunked. This paper aims to explain why and how I created a YouTube channel dedicated to Egyptology, and to present the conclusions I drew from this experience, both regarding Egyptology itself, as an academic field, and its relationship with the public. I will discuss the effectiveness of this practice, as well as its limitations, and what we can expect from the future of popularization of science, particularly History.

11. Murielle Richard, "Public Outreach through Serious Games: The Case of ArchéoSport," Bridging Ancient Worlds, 24 p. 10.5281/zenodo.22256808.

Abstract
Public outreach aims to make scientific knowledge accessible to the widest possible audience by fostering exchange, understanding and critical thinking. Among the innovative tools currently being used, serious games are playing an increasingly prominent role, thanks to their ability to combine learning and entertainment. By drawing on experimentation, motivation, social interaction and learning from mistakes, they enable participants to engage actively with knowledge. The game ArchéoSport, developed at the Université de Montpellier Paul-Valéry, illustrates this approach. Created as part of research into the archaeology of sport and the Paris 2024 Olympic Games, it invites participants to explore the evolution of sporting practices from the Neolithic period to the contemporary era. Using a timeline and cards depicting archaeological artefacts, players identify, date and interpret remains associated with ancient physical activities. Beyond the history of sport, the game raises awareness of archaeological methods, develops critical thinking, and addresses social issues such as the role of women, disability, and the religious, political and educational functions of sport. Developed collaboratively by researchers, museums and sports institutions, ArchéoSport demonstrates the potential of serious games as tools for public outreach. Despite the challenges involved in their design and in assessing their impact, such initiatives offer an effective approach to strengthening scientific literacy and bringing research closer to society.

12. Alicia Flores Martín, "A Year in the Life of Science Factory: Digital Outreach and Academic Networks in Public Egyptology," Bridging Ancient Worlds, 29 p. 10.5281/zenodo.23054934.

Abstract
This paper analyses the trajectory and development of the social media channel and videopodcast Science Factory during its first year of activity. After more than ten years, La Fábrica de la Ciencia, a Spanish-language video podcast, created an internationally oriented multilingual section. Unexpected technical issues associated with the production of the international interviews led to the creation of an independent platform: Science Factory. Although initially conceived as a twin project, the two channels soon diverged. Whereas La Fábrica de la Ciencia continued to cover a broad range of scientific topics, Science Factory became exclusively dedicated to Egyptology. The content was structured around three main areas: archaeological field work, museum work and academic research. This paper examines the development of Science Factory as both a digital outreach initiative and a case study in public archaeology and science communication. The analysis is based on a database compiled from YouTube analytics collected during the study period (between March 2025 and March 2026), allowing us to evaluate patterns, audience behaviour and content performance. The results suggest the following ideas: the first one shows that the project’s academic networks were essential for establishing its credibility and facilitating access to internationally recognized specialists; and the second one illustrate that quantitative analysis proves that short and long-form interviews, rather than competing with one another, fulfil complementary functions: Shorts increased discoverability, while interviews promoted deeper engagement with Egyptological research. The study argues that digital outreach should itself be considered an object of scholarly research. Systematic analysis of platform metrics provides valuable evidence for understanding how specialized archaeological knowledge circulates beyond academia and offers practical insights for the development of future outreach initiatives, including the successor project Egyptology Now.

13. José das Candeias Sales, "Exhibition TA-MERI: Terra Amada. Combining Art, Academic Knowledge and Artificial Intelligence," Bridging Ancient Worlds, 14 p. 10.5281/zenodo.23136050.

Abstract
This paper explores interdisciplinarity in the production of knowledge, highlighting the fusion of theoretical reflection, contemporary painting and artificial intelligence. The exhibition TA-MERI: Terra Amada (TA-MERI: Beloved Land) served as a practical model illustrating how collaboration between Egyptologists, painters and multimedia engineers can create new forms of cultural, artistic and pedagogical communication in the twenty-first century, thereby broadening the dissemination of research and the reception of ancient Egypt.
